Best Vinyl for Boat Lettering: Cast vs Calendared

Three decisions: cast or calendared, standard or high-tack, and whether you need a specialty finish. Get the second one wrong and your decal falls off.

Cast vs calendared

Cast vinyl is made by pouring liquid resin onto a carrier and letting it cure flat. Because it is never stretched, it carries no internal stress, so it conforms to curves and stays where you put it. Thinner, more conformable, longer outdoor life — typically 7 to 9 years.

Calendared vinyl is squeezed between rollers into sheet. It is cheaper and slightly thicker, but it remembers being stretched and slowly tries to shrink back, which is what makes old decals pull away at the corners. Typically 5 to 7 years, less on a dark colour in southern sun.

For a flat transom on a trailered boat, calendared is honestly fine and it is what we ship as standard. For a curved hull, a dark colour, or a boat kept in a slip in Florida, pay the $8.

High-tack: not optional on plastic

Polyethylene — rotomoulded kayaks, PWC hulls, some pontoon furniture — has a low surface energy, which is a technical way of saying almost nothing sticks to it. Standard vinyl will hold for a few weeks and then release in one clean piece.

High-tack film uses a much more aggressive adhesive designed for exactly this. We send it free on any kayak, canoe or PWC order, and on textured pontoon rails, because shipping the wrong film to save nothing is a bad trade.

What a 7-year warranty actually covers

Honest version: it covers the film — colour fade beyond a stated tolerance, cracking, and adhesive failure where the film was applied correctly to a suitable surface. It does not cover a decal applied over wax, applied in the cold, applied to a surface it was not specified for, or physically damaged.

Separately from the film warranty, we re-cut free if the install goes wrong. That is our policy, not the manufacturer’s, and it is the one most people actually end up using.
